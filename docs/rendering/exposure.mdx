title: Exposure
description: How a camera turns physical light values into a picture, the exposure modes, how Auto metering treats the sky, and how the Scene View sets its own exposure.

Lights in this engine use physical units: the sun is about 100,000 lux, a lamp a few hundred lumens. Exposure turns those values into a picture, the way a camera's aperture, shutter and sensitivity do. Exposure belongs to the **Camera** component; a Post Process Volume can only offset it (the **Exposure Adjustment** effect, in stops). The editor's Scene View has no camera and sets its own exposure; see [The Scene View's exposure](#the-scene-views-exposure).

## What the sun's lux means

A directional light's illuminance is what it delivers on the ground when it is straight overhead: a light at 100,000 lux puts 100,000 lux on a surface facing it with the sun straight overhead. While the sky drives the light (the default), the light's color carries the extra air a lower sun shines through, so it turns warmer and dimmer as the sun sinks; leave the intensity at its overhead value. A directional light that no sky drives keeps the color you give it. The physical sky lights its atmosphere from that same number. It lifts the value to what arrives above the atmosphere and scatters it from there, so the sun drawn in the sky delivers exactly the light's lux overhead, and the sky's own light adds to it. With the default atmosphere, a 100,000 lux sun at 60 degrees gives about 84,000 lux of direct light and 13,000 lux of skylight on the ground.

The default atmosphere is a crisp clear day: a deep blue zenith over a light haze that pales the sky toward the horizon and fills shadows with skylight. The ground below the horizon reflects light back into the sky too, by the sky's **Ground albedo ramp**. The atmosphere has no other settings yet, so **Exposure Compensation** is the way to brighten or darken it. A camera on **Manual** or **Physical** exposure keeps its number when the sky changes, and the Scene Light meter counts the skylight it measures: a scene exposed by hand against an earlier, brighter sky reads darker without notice. To re-meter it, switch the camera to **Auto**, note the metered EV it settles on, and type that number back into **Manual EV100**.

## Exposure modes

| Mode | What sets the exposure | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Auto** (default) | The camera meters the scene every frame and adapts over time, like an eye. | Games where the player moves between bright and dark places. |
| **Manual** | **Manual EV100**, an absolute EV100: 15 is bright sun, 12 overcast, 7 to 9 an interior, 2 to 5 night. | Shots that must not change with what is on screen. |
| **Physical** | Aperture, shutter time and ISO, as on a real camera (f/16, 1/100 s, ISO 100 is about EV 14.6). | Matching photographic reference. |
| **Fixed** | A plain linear multiplier. | Non-physical content. |

**Exposure Compensation** applies in every mode, in stops: +1 doubles the brightness, -1 halves it.

## How Auto metering works

Auto exposure looks at the pixels of the frame before bloom and tone mapping and sets the exposure so their average lands at 18 % gray, the middle gray of a photograph. Two rules keep it from being fooled:

- **The darkest and brightest tenth are ignored.** Deep shadows and specular highlights do not move the meter.
- **The sky is not part of the average.** The meter reads what the camera sees of the world: the ground, buildings and characters. Pixels where nothing was drawn (the sky, the sun, clouds) are counted apart. The sea is part of the world: an ocean surface is metered like the ground, so a coast, a ship or a flight over water is exposed for the water and what stands on it. A character standing against a bright sky is exposed for the character, not for the sky behind it.

The sky still has a say when it fills a large part of the frame. If its average would sit more than **two stops** over middle gray, it holds the exposure back so it does not burn to white. How much it holds back follows its share of the frame:

| Sky in view | Effect on exposure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Under 10 % | None: the scene is metered on its own and the sky may blow out into bloom. | A dark room with a daylight window; turning toward the window brightens the meter through adaptation. |
| 10 % to 30 % | Grows with the share. | A street with a strip of sky. |
| Over 30 % | Full: the sky's average sits at most two stops over middle gray. | A landscape at dusk; a model on a floor under the open sky. |
| 95 % to 100 % | The meter blends from the scene toward the sky as the scene shrinks below 5 % of the frame, and meters only the sky when nothing else is in view. | Looking up into open sky from a cockpit. |

:::note

What lands at middle gray is the average of the world in view (the middle 80 % of its brightness), not any one object. An 18 % gray card reads middle gray when the scene around it averages 18 % and the sky is not holding the exposure back. Two things move it away:

- **A bright sky over 30 % of the frame** (dawn, dusk, a model under the open sky): the sky's limit holds the exposure back, and the ground sits about half a stop under middle gray.
- **A floor or ground lighter than 18 %** (snow, sand, pale stone, a white studio floor): when the floor fills most of the view, the meter keys it to middle gray, so what stands on it reads darker by up to `log2(albedo / 0.18)` stops: typically up to about 1.7 for a floor of albedo 0.6 and 2.5 for a white (1.0) floor, and about 2.2 for the Default Material (0.8). The less of the view the floor fills, the smaller the offset (a model on a white floor under the open sky measured 0.7 to 1 stop). Raise **Exposure Compensation** by the offset you see to put an 18 % subject back at middle gray.

**Exposure Compensation** is the only control that moves the target.

:::

## Auto settings on the Camera

| Setting | Default | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Auto Min EV** | 4 | The darkest scene the meter fully brightens. Lower it to let night scenes brighten more. |
| **Auto Max EV** | 18 | The brightest scene the meter fully darkens. |
| **Auto Speed Up** | 1 | How fast the picture brightens when the scene gets darker (stops per second, roughly). |
| **Auto Speed Down** | 3 | How fast the picture darkens when the scene gets brighter. |

## The Scene View's exposure

The Scene View has no Camera component, so it chooses its exposure with its own **Mode**. The Game View always shows its camera's exposure; the two views may differ.

| Mode | What sets the exposure |
|---|---|
| **Scene Light** (default) | A fixed exposure derived from the light falling on a horizontal surface: every directional light's illuminance times the sine of its height above the horizon (a light below the horizon adds nothing), plus the sky's light on the ground, as an incident light meter reads it (`EV100 = log2(lux / 2.5)`; a 100,000 lux sun overhead reads 15.3). The meter counts the directional lights the renderer lights: the four strongest, as the renderer draws them; a fifth and weaker one adds nothing to the picture or the reading. It is worked out again whenever a directional light or the sky changes (time of day, latitude, a light's illuminance, a light added, removed, enabled or disabled, an atmosphere edit), never from the pixels on screen, so the picture does not drift while you move. A scene with no directional light and no sky uses EV 15.4. |
| **Auto** | The same meter as a camera in Auto (see above), with its own Min/Max EV and adaptation delay. |
| **Fixed** | The EV100 you set with **Fixed EV100**. Switching to Fixed from Scene Light or Auto starts it at the exposure the view was showing, so the picture does not jump. |

Change the mode from the Scene View toolbar's camera button (the **Exposure** section shows the derived EV under Scene Light), from the right-click menu on the toolbar's post-processing button, or in **Project Settings > Scene View > Exposure**. **EV Compensation** applies on top in every mode. Scene Light follows the light, not the materials: a scene of white or very dark surfaces is not pulled toward middle gray the way Auto pulls it.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everything reads dark in bright sun, and the ground is very light | The meter keys the average to middle gray; light materials (snow, sand, pale grass) average brighter than 18 %. | Check material base colors against real albedos (grass 0.1 to 0.25); or add positive Exposure Compensation. |
| Loading a scene reports a skipped `Camera.autoExposureKey` | The property no longer exists; the target is fixed at 18 % gray. | Delete the line; use `Camera.exposureCompensation` (stops) instead. |
